Imagine stocks fall sharply, your puts gain value, then stocks recover before expiry. The puts eventually expire worthless. Their terminal payoff alone wouldn’t capture the protection they provided during the selloff.
There are two distinct benefits to investigate:
Protection while holding: the puts’ increased value offsets some equity losses, cushioning portfolio NAV. You don’t have to sell them for that protection to exist.
Access to cash: selling some protection could realize gains to meet financing needs, rebalance, or purchase depressed assets. That requires an actual monetization rule—and executable prices.
Your suggestion of selling the hedge during the crash and buying protection again later adds another strategy layer. It might help, but it introduces difficult decisions: when to sell, how much protection to retain, and when to re-enter. Selling before another leg down could leave the portfolio exposed; replacement protection could also be expensive. We’d need rules specified beforehand.
Our study did include intrawindow marks and drawdown measures, so we didn’t entirely overlook protection along the way. But terminal tail loss drove selection, and we didn’t test early monetization or demonstrate usable liquidity from real option quotes.
So my question is narrower than “we evaluated it incorrectly”: would a different protection objective reveal useful benefits that the terminal-loss objective doesn’t establish?
